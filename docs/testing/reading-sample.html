The Lighthouse at Cape Mercy

Published on a quiet Tuesday morning

The keeper had lived alone on the headland for eleven winters, and in that time he had learned to read the weather the way other people read a newspaper. A certain flatness in the light meant fog before evening. Gulls turning inland meant a gale within the day.

Every night he climbed the ninety-four steps, trimmed the wick, and watched the beam sweep out across water that was never quite the same colour twice. The ships passed far out, their lights small and patient, and he counted them the way a shepherd counts a flock he will never touch.

What the supply boat brought

Once a month a boat came around the point with flour, lamp oil, and letters that were often three weeks old by the time they reached him. He read each one twice: the first time quickly, for the news, and the second time slowly, for the handwriting.

  • Flour, salt, and dried fish
  • Oil for the lamp
  • Letters from the mainland
  • Newspapers, always out of date

The winter the ice came

In the eighth winter the sea froze further out than anyone could remember. The supply boat could not reach the point for nine weeks. He rationed the oil, burning the lamp at half brightness, and told himself that a dim light was still a light.

When the thaw finally came, the first thing he saw through the glass was the boat, small and black against the grey, moving slowly through water that had forgotten how to be solid.
